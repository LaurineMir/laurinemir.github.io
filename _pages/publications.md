## Work in Progress

* **The gap between desired and realized fertility: interpretation and policy implications** with [Pauline Rossi](https://sites.google.com/site/paulinerossimoulin/home) and French Council of Economic Analysis [CAE](http://cae-eco.fr)

<font size="2"> <p style="line-height: 1.4; text-align: justify; margin-left: 55px;">
<strong><em>Abstract: </em></strong> 
In low-fertility countries, desired fertility exceeds realized fertility: people report wanting more children than they ultimately have. Policymakers invoke this gap to justify pro-natalist policies, on the premise that closing it is both feasible and legitimate. Yet the measurement and interpretation of desired fertility remain contested. In this paper, we use a discrete choice experiment to measure the trade-offs individuals are willing to make - in terms of consumption, leisure, housing, and career progression - in order to have another child. This methodology allows us to distinguish between what people anticipate having to give up when a child is born and what they are actually willing to do. We implement the methodology on a sample of 2,800 individuals representative of the French population aged 20 to 45. We find that fertility intentions are primarily determined by preferences rather than constraints: individuals who intend to move closer to their ideal number of children are those who are willing to make greater sacrifices, not those for whom children are less costly. Policy simulations suggest that relaxing these trade-offs would lead only to modest gains in aggregate fertility and may be detrimental to welfare once the fiscal cost is taken into account.
</p> </font>

* **Polygamy as a Ponzi Scheme : how population growthr rates shapes marriage market in Africa** with [Pauline Rossi](https://sites.google.com/site/paulinerossimoulin/home)

<font size="2"> <p style="line-height: 1.4; text-align: justify; margin-left: 55px;">
<strong><em>Abstract: </em></strong> 
In Sub-Saharan Africa, polygamy is very prevalent : 10 to 50% of married women are in a polygamous union depending on the country. Yet, historically, the fraction of men who never marry has always been low. This is because a fast population growth combined with a large age gap between husbands and wives created a surplus of women on the marriage market. However, this surplus is projected to shrink as population growth has started to slow down. The demographic conditions necessary to sustain high levels of polygamy while ensuring that all men eventually marry are therefore less likely to hold in the future. What will be the effect on the marriage market? Will polygamy disappear or will male celibacy explode? We develop a structural model to study under which conditions we should observe a decrease in the share of polygamous men and/or an increase in the share of single men when population growth declines. We provide an empirical application using original data from Senegal.
</p> </font>
